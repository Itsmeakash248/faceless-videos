## 2. Description

In March 1968, Soviet ballistic-missile submarine K-129 vanished 1,560 miles northwest of Hawaii with three nuclear warheads and roughly eighty crewmen. 

The Soviet Navy launched a massive two-month search across thousands of square miles and found nothing. 

Unknown to Moscow, the US Navy's SOSUS acoustic network recorded the precise acoustic signature of K-129's catastrophic implosion 16,500 feet below the Pacific surface—over three miles straight down into the pitch-black oceanic abyss.

What followed was the most audacious covert salvage operation in Cold War history: **Project Azorian**. 

Backed by Howard Hughes under the elaborate cover of commercial deep-sea manganese nodule mining, the CIA spent between $350 and $500 million (over $2.5 billion in today's money) building the 600-foot *Hughes Glomar Explorer*. Amidships stood a 236-foot derrick and a secret moon pool hidden beneath the waterline. 

From the belly of the ship, engineers lowered a 100-foot mechanical capture claw ("Clementine") on a three-mile string of heavy steel pipe into the crushing darkness. On the seabed, the massive hydraulic tines closed around K-129's 1,750-ton forward hull section. 

Then came the disaster: roughly halfway to the surface, under 16,000 feet of hydrostatic pressure, two giant tines suffered catastrophic stress fractures. The main hull broke apart—plunging the conning tower, ballistic missiles, and code machines back into the abyss. 

Only a 38-foot bow section reached the surface, containing two nuclear torpedoes and the bodies of six Soviet submariners, who were subsequently buried at sea with full military honours.

When the secret operation leaked to the press in 1975, the CIA refused to answer reporters, creating the legendary legal doctrine: *"We can neither confirm nor deny"* (The Glomar response).
